# On-set checklist

Cine Light Match can work from almost nothing, but every item below gives it more reliable information.

### Quick checklist

Do these for each setup, in this order of value:

- [ ] Take a photo with your phone from the camera position, pointing in the same direction as the camera
- [ ] Note the camera model, lens, ISO, shutter, aperture, and time of day
- [ ] Shoot a 360° HDRI, or at least a gray ball and a chrome ball
- [ ] Put a reference object with a crisp shadow in frame for a few seconds
- [ ] Use a tripod for shots with a moving practical light
- [ ] Keep lens distortion low, or shoot a lens grid

### What each item gives you

| Item | What Cine Light Match uses it for |
| --- | --- |
| Phone photo | Places the sun from location, date, time, and heading |
| Camera notes | Matches exposure and builds the matched camera |
| HDRI | Aligns the sun automatically and sets sky lighting |
| Gray and chrome balls | Finds light direction and key-to-fill balance |
| Reference shadow | Gives the light solver a clean shadow clue |
| Tripod | Keeps moving-light solves simple and accurate |
| Lens grid | Lets clues line up correctly in the plate |

### 1. Phone photo from the camera position

Take a photo with your phone from the camera position, pointing in the same direction as the camera

- Turn on location for the camera app first, or the photo won't store GPS or compass heading
- Cinema cameras rarely record location, so this photo is often the only source
- One photo per setup is enough, since the sun doesn't move noticeably during a single take

### 2. Camera notes

Write down, for each setup:

- camera model, or sensor size if it's a non-standard mode
- lens focal length
- ISO, shutter speed or shutter angle, and aperture
- time of day and time zone

Set the camera's clock to the correct local time before the shoot. Many cameras store time without a time zone.

### 3. HDRI or reference balls

A 360° HDRI is the best lighting reference. Shoot it from where the CG object will sit, as close in time to the take as you can. The sun moves about 15 degrees an hour, so an HDRI shot much later won't match.

If you can't shoot an HDRI, film a gray ball and a chrome ball instead:

- Hold them where the CG object will be.
- Keep the whole ball in frame and in focus.
- Hold for a few seconds so there's a clean frame to use.

The chrome ball shows where lights are. The gray ball shows how strong the key is compared with the fill.

### 4. Reference shadow object

Place something with a sharp, clear shadow in frame for a few seconds, ideally on the ground near where the CG object will go. A light stand, a pole, or a box all work. If you can, measure its height.

A person standing still also works: their feet and the shadow of their head make a strong clue.

### 5. Tripod for moving practical lights

For shots where a light moves, such as a flashlight or a car's headlights, lock the camera off on a tripod. Moving-camera support is planned for a later version. Until then, a locked-off camera gives the best results.

### 6. Lens distortion

Wide lenses bend straight lines, which throws off shadow clues. Either:

- Keep distortion low by avoiding very wide lenses for VFX shots, or
- Shoot a lens grid: a printed grid filling the frame, at the same focal length and focus distance as the shot.

### What to bring

- Phone with location turned on
- Notebook, or a notes app, for camera details
- 360° camera or HDRI rig, if available
- Gray ball and chrome ball
- A light stand or pole for reference shadows
- Tape measure
- Printed lens grid
- Tripod

### After the shoot

Keep the original files. Messaging apps and some cloud services strip location and time data from photos, so copy phone photos straight from the phone or export them as originals.

Store each setup's reference together: the phone photo, the camera notes, the HDRI or ball frames, and the plate. Name folders by scene and setup so the right reference is easy to find later.
